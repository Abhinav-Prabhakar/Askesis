Skeptical Theism and Evil

Evil is evidence against God — unless our ignorance is the evidence.

2 MIN · EDITED
PHILOSOPHY OF RELIGION · GRIEFHorror doesn't look like a greater good.The sceptic's reply: it wouldn't look like one to us anyway.
IN ONE BREATH

Rowe points to a fawn burning in a forest fire: pointless suffering, no soul-making in sight. Skeptical theists reply that our grasp of goods is tiny next to God's; absence of a visible reason isn't absence of a reason.

The tension

Suffering feels gratuitous, and a good God would prevent gratuitous suffering. But 'looks gratuitous to us' is a claim about us, not about the universe. The debate turns on how much weight human ignorance should carry.

Three positions

R
01
ROWE

Pointless evil exists — the fawn, the child. A good God would stop it. So no such God exists.

W
02
WYKSTRA

CORNEA: we should expect God's reasons to be beyond our ken, the way a parent's reasons escape a toddler.

D
03
DRAPER

Pain and pleasure look exactly as we'd expect with no God — and surprising with one. Biology, not theodicy.

THE ARGUMENT, IN THREE LINES
P1If God had a justifying reason for horrific evil, we have no reason to expect we would grasp it.
P2We cannot grasp any such reason for cases like the burning fawn.
∴Therefore our inability to see a reason is not evidence there is none. (The skeptical-theist block.)

The strongest objection

Skepticism spreads: if our moral vision is that dim, we lose ordinary moral knowledge too — and theism itself. This is the Pandora's box objection, and skeptical theists spend most effort containing it.

“My ways are not your ways.”
Isaiah 55:8 · often cited in the debate
