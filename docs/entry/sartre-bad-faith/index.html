Sartre's Bad Faith

You always have a choice — bad faith is pretending you don't.

2 MIN · EDITED
EXISTENTIALISM · MODERNYou are free — even when you pretend not to be.The waiter, the date, the delay. Three scenes of self-deception.
IN ONE BREATH

Sartre says we lie to ourselves to dodge our freedom. The waiter plays at being a waiter; the woman on a date pretends she can't see what's happening. In each case the person knows, and doesn't know, at the same time.

The tension

Freedom is absolute, yet life feels constrained. We have jobs, pasts, other people's expectations. Sartre's tension: you are shaped by all of this, and still the next move is yours. is the trick of treating yourself as an object — a role, a victim, a thing with no choice — so the weight lifts for a moment.

THE ARGUMENT, IN THREE LINES
P1If you are conscious, you stand apart from every role and fact about yourself — you can always imagine otherwise.
P2To treat yourself as fixed by a role ('I had no choice, I'm just the waiter') denies what consciousness is.
∴Therefore — denying your own freedom to yourself — is the fundamental self-deception.

The strongest objection

OBJECTION

The strongest objection is the paradox itself: the deceiver and the deceived are the same person, so someone must know the truth all along. Sartre answers that consciousness is slippery — it can hold the truth and look away at once.

VS
REPLY

Slippery isn't broken — consciousness can hold the truth and look away at once. The paradox describes the mechanism; it doesn't refute it.
